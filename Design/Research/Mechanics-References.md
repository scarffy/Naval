# Mechanics References for Naval

Research notes on Railway Empire (1 and 2) and comparable trading games, to inform Naval's port stock, port upgrades, and multi-year missions.

**Reliability note.** WebFetch could not reach any site in this environment (gamepressure.com, magicgameworld.com and steamcommunity.com all failed to resolve), so everything below comes from search-result summaries of guides, reviews, patch notes and Steam forum posts. Most numbers are community-reported. Anything I could not confirm is marked "(unverified)". Treat exact figures as leads to check in the games, not as facts.

---

## 1. Railway Empire 1 and 2

### Station buildings and warehouses
- A station can take a small number of extension buildings. Players report a hard cap of **two extensions per station** and complain about it. One player lists three extensions that raise express-train revenue (two for passengers, one for mail). Exact costs: not found (unverified).
- In RE2 you select a station and press "Construct Extension" to see the menu. Warehouses and inns are built this way. RE2 reviewers note stations can now be built anywhere, and one player says warehouses now live only in stations (in RE1 they were separate buildings).
- Warehouses are small stores used to move goods between industries and cities. Players report a **limit of about 6 items** per warehouse (RE1 forum, repeated for RE2) and ask for more. Warehouses in city stations can swap goods with other warehouses.
- RE2 Update 1.7 (24 June 2025) added a new warehouse operating mode. One player summary says that with automatic distribution off, a warehouse can act as a permanent store (the patch announcement confirms a new mode; the "permanent store" detail is from a player post, partly unverified).
- Track capacity is the other station "limit": guides advise upgrading only choke-point stations from two to four tracks, not every line.
- Lesson for us: the player-facing limit is a few slots, not a number that grows forever. This keeps decisions meaningful.

### Cities: demand and growth
- Each city shows a demand tab listing goods it wants. Goods only load for a destination that wants them.
- Demand grows with population. Players report the first handful of consumer goods are demanded from the start and more unlock as the city passes population steps (one post says 5,000-person steps; another says full demand needs roughly 110,000 to 120,000 people) (both unverified).
- Growth rule as reported by players: a city grows when it gets about **60% of its consumer-goods demand**, judged as a weighted average over about 30 days. RE2 players report **80%** gives faster growth. Passengers and mail alone do not grow a city. Industrial goods do not count. Export goods are said to have little effect (all unverified, from forum posts).
- Disagreement exists on growth pace; one player says small cities grow faster in relative terms and that a quoted "about 6% a month" does not match what they see.
- So yes: supplying a city makes it grow, and a bigger city demands more goods and more kinds of goods. That is the main engine of late-game scale.

### Rural industries and farms
- Industries (farms, mines, factories) are bought and upgraded. Upgrades raise storage for both inputs and outputs, according to a forum user. A guide says a second factory can be built once a city reaches level 3 (about 40,000 inhabitants).
- Production runs at full rate until local stock is full, then stops. A factory whose products are not collected or used stops accepting deliveries. This is the "storage cap as a brake" idea in its simplest form.
- A forum thread cites an in-game hint that industries need roughly 60% utilisation to be profitable (RE1; unverified for RE2).
- A player reports that an owned business you starve of pickups goes bankrupt and its sale price falls, so neglect has a price (anecdotal).
- Upgrade costs and per-month output: not found (unverified). RE2's official guidebook ships with the game and probably has the tables.

### Prices
- Freight pay appears **flat per wagon**, not tied to distance or speed. Guides say about 2,000 per wagon in RE2 and in RE1 (unverified exact figure). Staff, the tech tree and special wagons modify this. Finished goods pay more than raw materials.
- Passengers and mail pay more with distance. RE2 passenger fares are tied to travel time, so slow freight cars attached to a passenger train can lower income.
- I found no evidence of price falling from oversupply, and one player says demand is steady so oversupply is not a worry. In effect, prices are fixed and the **limit is demand and storage**, not price movement. This is the closest existing precedent for Naval's Beginner mode.

### Time
- RE1 eras run about 20 years each, and a standard game is usually over at the end of an era (scenario objectives are tailored to roughly 20 or 100 years). Players can continue but stop earning research points. One player says you can start in 1870 and play 60 years.
- Real minutes per in-game year: not found (unverified). RE1 mods mention a "global speed factor" (about 4.5 in one scenario) but I could not convert that to minutes.
- Time matters mostly through technology: new trains and research unlock with the calendar, and trains age and break down (RE2 added an option in 1.7 to turn off small breakdowns).

### Scenarios, campaign and RE2 changes
- RE1 campaign scenarios do not carry over state. Each starts afresh and grants a perk (for example a freight revenue bonus of about 5% or a top-speed boost).
- RE2: five campaign chapters. Sources disagree on scenario count (14 or 16, unverified). In scenario mode all objectives are mandatory and the game is a race against a rival mogul; in campaign mode some objectives are optional. Completing objectives opens a new region for expansion, which gives pacing.
- RE2 changes: simpler signalling (later patched to bring back manual signals in Update 1.1), stations anywhere, co-op multiplayer, a broader audience and, per reviewers, a little less depth.
- Typical stated pressures: rival moguls, a limited number of years, running costs and breakdowns, train ageing. I could not confirm numbers for upkeep or rising costs (unverified). Several players say money stops mattering once the network is built.

### Critical view
- Reviews describe RE2's late game as labour-intensive and repetitive: many routes to manage, delivering late-game goods, and weak train AI. One summary says the loop gets repetitive quickly. Mixed-positive overall (examples: 8.5/10 from one outlet, 7/10 from another).

## 2. Other games

### Port Royale 3 and 4
- **Prices:** dynamic. Each commodity has a supply or stock indicator per town (PR4 shows it as bars). High supply means low price; heavy deliveries push the price down; strong consumption pushes it up.
- **Stock and warehouses:** PR4 warehouses come in tiers. One guide lists small (5,000 items, 1,000 gold/day), medium (10,000, 2,000/day) and large (20,000, 3,000/day) (unverified; mixed-content source). You can set warehouses to buy and sell goods with the town. A forum post says PR4 lost the older per-good stock-protection setting.
- **Growth:** settler growth depends on supply and satisfaction. A PR3 forum post says growth bonuses shrink as towns get bigger unless you build more, and treasure fleets are the main immigrant source. Production buildings turn cheap goods into more tradable goods.
- **Time:** four campaigns (England, Spain, Netherlands, France); years pass and the colonial balance shifts. Campaign length in years: not found (unverified). Hurricanes: not confirmed.
- **Brakes:** price falling as you sell; warehouse daily rent; wars and pirates.
- **Criticism:** after roughly 15 hours "everything is repetition"; trading becomes a waiting game while numbers grow; simplistic trading lets you snowball; automatic routes are tedious to set up and sometimes unprofitable. Steam summary "Mixed", about 2,000 reviews.

### Patrician III and IV
- **Prices:** dynamic and tied to town size, prosperity and stock on the market. In P3, a large stock makes the price climb slowly to a critical point (as described by a forum poster). The manual suggests a buy/sell gap of about 10% to avoid shortages.
- **Warehouses:** the player rents or builds storage in each town; guides warn that renting space eats profit fast, so size it to the time between ship visits.
- **Growth:** the player builds housing and businesses that grow the town; profitability partly comes from the growth they trigger (P2 tip).
- **Brakes and pressure:** rival traders (price wars, sabotage), pirates as you grow, and rank in the Hanse league as a goal.
- **Criticism:** a "solid game of trade that loses its momentum early due to heavy repetition"; naval combat is dumbed down. A reviewer says the long game stays rewarding if you keep founding cities and doing quests.

### Uncharted Waters (New Horizons, Online, Origin)
- **Prices:** dynamic, per port. Flooding a port with one good drops its price, and Origin labels trends "trending" (rising) or "overflowing" (falling). In New Horizons, a price index of about 140% or more can make a specialty good vanish until prices fall (unverified detail from a guide).
- **Port investment:** you invest gold in a port over time, making it a source of better ships or a certain trade good. This is a long-term, player-chosen money sink that changes the map.
- **Time:** years pass with the story, and goods and ships vary by era (details unverified).

### Anno 1800 trade routes
- Trade routes are set between your own islands (charter) or with AI or neutral parties (needs a diplomatic agreement). Routes can loop. A **minimum stock** setting stops a route emptying the source island, which is a useful idea for us.
- Prices in the trading post show buy and sell prices with a history button. Trade Union items can partly offset shortages. Details on limits: not found (unverified).
- Growth comes from supplying residents with goods in tiers; populations unlock the next tier. (General knowledge, not confirmed in my searches.)

## 4. Ideas for Naval

Complexity: Low, Medium, High. Difficulty: B = Beginner (fixed prices), V = Veteran (reactive market).

### Port capacity and upgrades
1. **Stock slots per good with visible crates.** Each port has a stock number (for example 12 crates) per good, shown on the port screen. Selling beyond the stock sells at nothing (or is refused); stock restocks on a daily curve. *Effect:* the brake for fixed prices; the player can read why a route saturates. *Complexity:* Low. *Difficulty:* B (works for V as the cap behind prices).
2. **Port tiers with a few slots, not unlimited growth.** A port has two or three "extension" slots (Warehouse: more crates held and bought; Market Hall: faster restock; Customs House: cheaper dues). Each slot costs more than the last and is permanent. *Effect:* a Railway Empire-style choice, since you cannot buy everything, and it gives gold a use. *Complexity:* Medium. *Difficulty:* Both.
3. **Capacity upgrades cost money to hold (upkeep).** Each extension has a monthly gold cost, so a half-used warehouse is a bad buy (borrowed from Port Royale rent and RE's utilisation idea). *Effect:* stops "buy everything" and rewards matching capacity to actual routes. *Complexity:* Low. *Difficulty:* Both.
4. **Minimum-stock setting on your own goods** (from Anno): you choose how many crates stay at a port before your ships may buy more. *Effect:* helps planning for chain goods. *Complexity:* Low-Medium. *Difficulty:* B.
5. **Veteran price pressure on top of stock.** Each crate sold lowers the price a little, recovering over days; stock depletes as well. *Effect:* two interacting brakes, so Veteran feels different from Beginner and is not just harder. *Complexity:* Medium. *Difficulty:* V.

### Port growth
6. **Ports grow when their needs are met.** Each port has a few "wanted" goods; keeping them supplied (for example more than 60% of weekly demand, borrowed from RE) raises port level, which raises stock caps and unlocks new goods. *Effect:* supplying a port is the reward, as in Railway Empire. *Complexity:* Medium. *Difficulty:* Both.
7. **Growth caps by tier and decay if neglected.** Level ceilings tied to harbour size, and a port slides back if starved for a season. *Effect:* prevents infinite growth and adds maintenance pressure. *Complexity:* Medium. *Difficulty:* V (optional in B).
8. **Investment projects** (from Uncharted Waters): pay gold to turn a port into a specialist (shipyard, sugar source). *Effect:* a long-term gold sink with a visible map result. *Complexity:* Medium. *Difficulty:* Both.

### Multi-year time scales
9. **Seasonal calendar per year.** Hurricane summer closes or damages some routes; harvest season raises restock of farm goods; winter slows some goods. Players plan around a year-long cycle. *Complexity:* Medium. *Difficulty:* Both (B shows the forecast; V hides it partly).
10. **Mission stages tied to years.** Each year sets one or two objectives (for example "export 200 crates of sugar by end of 1771"), and finishing them unlocks a new region or route to buy, like RE2's opening regions. *Complexity:* Medium. *Difficulty:* Both.
11. **Pause and fast-forward at sea** with a yearly summary screen (profit, best route, rival standing). *Complexity:* Low. *Difficulty:* Both.

### Mid-game variety
12. **Contracts with deadlines and penalties** from governors or merchants ("deliver 20 crates of rum to Port X by June; failing costs reputation"). *Effect:* breaks the pure buy-low sell-high loop. *Complexity:* Medium. *Difficulty:* Both.
13. **Rival merchant companies** who buy up port stock and take routes (Patrician-style competition without sabotage at first). *Effect:* your best route may be taken, so you adapt. *Complexity:* High. *Difficulty:* V (a light version in B).
14. **Random events with a clear cause**: war, bad harvest, a plague, pirate raids that change stock and demand for a few weeks. Show them in advance when possible. *Complexity:* Medium. *Difficulty:* V mainly.
15. **Unlockable goods and ships by year** (tech-tree-like). New goods unlock as ports level up, in the way RE unlocks goods with city size. *Complexity:* Low-Medium. *Difficulty:* Both.

### Money sinks
16. **Ship ageing and repair.** Ships need yearly refit; old ships are slower and sink more often (from RE train ageing). *Complexity:* Medium. *Difficulty:* Both.
17. **Port dues, crew wages and insurance** that scale with fleet size. *Effect:* growth costs more per ship. *Complexity:* Low. *Difficulty:* Both.
18. **Route licences** (you "buy sea routes" already): later routes cost more and need port level. *Complexity:* Low. *Difficulty:* Both.
19. **Prestige purchases**: a lighthouse, a church or a fort that give a permanent small bonus and show on the map. *Complexity:* Low. *Difficulty:* Both.

### Pitfalls the reviews point to
- Automatic routes that are tedious to set up (Port Royale 4) mean we should give copy-route and template tools.
- Games with simple, safe trading let you snowball and then wait (PR4). We need contracts, seasons and rivals before the player is rich.
- Late-game "many routes to manage" (RE2) argues for grouping ships into fleets with one set of orders.
