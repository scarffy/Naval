# Trade Goods of the 1770s: Research for Naval, Chapter 1

Scope: the 12 Chapter 1 ports, about 1765-1780. Method: a handful of web searches (sources in section 6), plus general historical knowledge. Anything not confirmed by a source I actually read is marked "(unverified)". Search results were mostly summaries and no source gave port-level 1770s customs figures, so the port tables are qualitative and should be treated as design inputs rather than data.

A note on slavery: sugar, coffee, indigo, tobacco, rice and rum all depended on enslaved labour in this period. Bridgetown, Port-au-Prince, Havana, New Orleans, Liverpool and Bordeaux were all involved in the slave trade, as shipping or financing ports. Liverpool and Bordeaux were major slaving ports; Liverpool's cargoes for Africa included Birmingham guns, Lancashire textiles and Staffordshire copper and brass. This research proposes no mechanic or trade good that involves people. If the game wants to acknowledge it, a codex or port-description note is enough.

## 1. Port-by-port table

| Port | Controlled by (c.1770) | Main exports | Main imports | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Natchez | Britain (West Florida) 1763-79; Spain from 1779 | Tobacco (first plantation crop), indigo, rice, timber products (timber was the most profitable export, per Encyclopedia of Alabama), deerskins | Cloth, hardware, rum, powder, provisions (unverified) | A small river settlement, not a deep-sea port. Goods went down the Mississippi to New Orleans or Pensacola/Mobile. Natchez merchants also handled the Indian trade. No cotton export in the 1770s; the gin is 1793. |
| New Orleans | Spain, from 1763 (formal takeover 1766-69, after the 1768 revolt) | Deerskins and furs, indigo, lumber, rice, naval stores (unverified), some tobacco | Textiles, wine and brandy, hardware, flour, enslaved people (not a game good) | Louisiana was opened to Spanish "free trade" ports in 1768 (per encyclopedia.com). Sugar was minor until the 1790s (unverified). Smuggling with British West Florida was common. |
| Campeche | Spain (New Spain, Yucatan) | Logwood, hides, wax, salt, coarse cotton cloth (the last three unverified) | Wine, oil, flour, iron goods, cloth, paper | Centre of the Spanish logwood trade. Yucatan and Campeche joined the free-trade area in 1770 and Campeche was classed a minor port in 1778 with lower duties (core.ac.uk sources). Small vessels carried logwood to Veracruz. British cutters at the Bay of Honduras competed and smuggled. |
| Veracruz | Spain (New Spain's main Atlantic port) | Silver and Spanish dollars (the largest by value), cochineal and indigo (from Oaxaca and Guatemala), vanilla, sugar, logwood re-shipped from Campeche | Spanish and European textiles, quicksilver (mercury for silver mines), wine, oil, paper, iron, flour, cacao (New Spain was the world's main cacao buyer, per core.ac.uk) | Before 1778 the fleet (flota) system funnelled trade via Cadiz. Free trade (1778-83) is covered by an Ulua article. Coffee was not a Veracruz export in 1770 (unverified; Cordoba and Veracruz coffee is a later growth). |
| Havana | Spain (British occupation 1762-63) | Sugar (rising fast), tobacco (royal monopoly leaf bought by the crown), hides, mahogany and ship timber, silver stopover | Flour, textiles, wine, hardware, salt cod, enslaved people (not a game good) | The main Spanish naval yard and fleet assembly point. Open to wider trade after 1765 and 1778. Tobacco went mostly to Seville and Cadiz factories (unverified detail). |
| Port-au-Prince | France (Saint-Domingue) | Sugar, indigo, coffee (rising in the 1770s), cotton, hides | Flour, wine, salt cod, salt beef, lumber, cloth, tools, enslaved people (not a game good) | The French exclusif forced colonial trade through French ports, but New England lumber and fish arrived by smuggling or licence (unverified in detail). Saint-Domingue was the biggest sugar and coffee producer in the world by the 1780s (unverified for 1770). |
| Bridgetown | Britain (Barbados) | Sugar, molasses, rum | Salt fish, flour and corn, lumber, cattle and horses, manufactured goods | British West Indian planters shipped nearly 100,000 tons of sugar and about 2 million gallons of rum a year in the 1770s. Barbadian rum exports virtually stopped with the war, and an early-1770s drought hurt the island (business Barbados, ouramericanrevolution). Barbados was old and soil-worn by 1770, so it imported a lot of food. |
| New York | Britain (colony) | Flour, wheat, bread, staves, pig and bar iron (more from New Jersey and Pennsylvania), furs from the Hudson and Albany trade | British cloth and hardware, sugar and molasses, rum, tea, wine | Enumerated goods rules forced some goods through Britain. Strongly tied to West Indian provisioning. |
| Boston | Britain (colony) | Salt cod, rum (distilled from imported molasses), whale oil, lumber, ships, some furs | Molasses and sugar, manufactured goods, cloth, hardware, wine, tea | The Sugar Act (1764) halved the molasses duty but actually enforced it, and trade slumped (Wikipedia). Tea Act 1773 led to the Tea Party; the Boston Port Act followed in 1774. Naval stores such as pitch came mainly from the Carolinas, not New England (unverified). |
| Liverpool | Britain | Manufactures: Birmingham guns and hardware, Lancashire and Yorkshire textiles, Cheshire salt, copper and brass | Tobacco, sugar, rum, raw cotton (small), timber, naval stores | Overtook Bristol in the Atlantic trade; its African cargoes depended on Birmingham guns, Lancashire textiles and Staffordshire metals (Gresham, Birmingham thesis). Salt as an export is not confirmed in my sources (unverified). |
| Bordeaux | France | Wine and brandy, flour and wine for Saint-Domingue (about 4.5 million livres of wine and 4.8 million of flour in 1788, per one secondary source), linens and textiles | Sugar, coffee, cotton and indigo from the Caribbean; salt cod; Irish salted beef (unverified) | Merchants owned and financed plantations. Colonial sugar was re-exported to Holland and Germany; wine went to Hamburg and the Baltic. Another port, La Rochelle, specialised more in re-exports. |
| Seville | Spain | Wine and olive oil (from the Jerez and Andalusian hinterland), oranges (bitter oranges for marmalade, unverified for exports), snuff and tobacco products from the royal factory (unverified), wool | Colonial tobacco, cochineal and silver through Cadiz, salt cod, hardware | The American monopoly moved from Seville to Cadiz in 1717 and lasted to 1778. Seville's share of fleet wine shipments fell from 77% (1717) to 15% (1757). By the 1770s Seville was a river port and manufacturing city under Cadiz, not the gateway it was in the 1500s. The 1778 comercio libre ended the Cadiz monopoly. |

## 2. Goods catalogue

### 2(a) The current 12 goods

| Good | Verdict | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Oranges | Keep, narrow | Plausible for Seville (and Valencia), and a perishable cargo works well mechanically. But it is not a big Atlantic trade good in 1770. Keep as a minor perishable, or swap for "Wine and Oil". |
| Sugar | Keep | The most valuable bulk commodity. Correct for Bridgetown, Port-au-Prince, Havana. Weak for New Orleans and Campeche in 1770 (unverified: Louisiana sugar took off in the 1790s). |
| Pitch | Rename | "Naval Stores" (pitch, tar, turpentine) is the real category, mainly from the Carolinas, with some from Louisiana and West Florida. Boston is not the source (unverified). Better merged into "Lumber & Naval Stores". |
| Cotton | Replace | Not a significant export of Natchez or New Orleans before the gin (1793) and Louisiana cotton boom. Some cotton came from the West Indies (Saint-Domingue, Barbados) but low volume. Use Indigo as the Gulf cash crop and Cloth as a Liverpool and Bordeaux export. |
| Tobacco | Keep | Correct for Natchez (British period), Havana, and Virginia/Maryland (not in the 12 ports). Liverpool and Seville were consumers/processors. |
| Iron | Keep or merge | Pig and bar iron did go from the Middle Colonies to Britain. For gameplay, merge into Hardware to avoid two similar goods. |
| Rum | Keep | Correct for Bridgetown and Boston (Boston distilled from imported molasses). Also New York and Medford, Newport (not in the 12 ports). |
| Furs | Keep, rename | "Furs & Deerskins". Deerskins from the Gulf were a major export, as the Panton, Leslie firm shows (32,000 skins in stock in 1776). Beaver and other furs came via New York. |
| Coffee | Keep, move | Veracruz is wrong for 1770. Port-au-Prince is right and Bordeaux a key re-export. |
| Steel | Replace | "Hardware" (tools, nails, ironware, cutlery) is the better category and what Birmingham, Sheffield and Liverpool shipped. Steel was a smaller, niche item. |
| Spices | Replace for Ch. 1 | Pepper and cloves reached Europe via Dutch, British and French East India companies. Bordeaux and Seville were not spice centres. Fine as a Chapter 2 or 3 good. For Chapter 1 use Wine. |
| Guns | Keep as event/limited, or merge into Hardware | Birmingham made trade guns that went through Liverpool, much of it to West Africa for the slave trade (Birmingham thesis; Journal of African History). Bordeaux as a gun exporter is unsupported (unverified). To avoid the slave-trade link, treat guns as a low-volume military and hunting good, or drop it. |

## 4. Mechanics ideas

| Idea | Historical basis | Gameplay effect | Complexity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Perishable cargo (spoilage timer) | Flour spoiled in tropical heat and damp; fruit and meat rot; salt cod and wine keep | Rewards short, fast routes for Oranges and Flour; slow routes for Cod and Rum. Adds a reason to pick faster ships | Low |
| Bulk vs value (hold space) | Logwood and lumber were bulky and cheap; cochineal and indigo compact and costly | Cargo weight vs value makes ship size matter; small ships carry luxury goods, big ships carry bulk | Low |
| Spanish port restriction and smuggling | Cadiz monopoly 1717-78; foreign ships barred from Spanish ports; widespread smuggling (Campeche logwood, Veracruz) | Foreign players pay a penalty or risk confiscation in Spanish ports; smuggling gives better prices with a chance of inspection | Medium |
| Comercio libre (1778) | 12 Oct 1778 regulation opened more Spanish ports and ended the Cadiz monopoly | A mid-game event: more ports and lower duties for Spanish goods | Low |
| Navigation Acts and enumerated goods | British colonial goods like sugar and tobacco had to go through British ports | British colonies sell some goods only via British ports; extra profit for legal routes, penalty for ignoring them | Medium |
| Sugar Act 1764 | Molasses duty halved but strictly collected; trade fell | A duty on foreign molasses at British ports; a choice between legal and smuggled supply | Medium |
| Tea Act 1773 and boycotts | Tea Party in Dec 1773; Coercive Acts 1774; later non-importation | A timed event that shuts or taxes some ports (Boston, then New York); price spikes for British goods in colonies | Medium |
| Seasonal harvests | Sugar crop season in the Caribbean was roughly January to June (unverified); tobacco shipped after autumn harvest (unverified) | Prices drop in harvest season at the source, giving a timing game | Medium |
| Production chain (molasses to rum) | New England distilleries turned imported molasses into rum | A port converts one good into another for a margin; adds a trade loop | Medium |
| War disruption | 1775-83 war; Spain joined 1779 (and Natchez changed hands); Barbados rum exports collapsed | Events close routes, raise insurance, and change port control. Pair with letters of marque if wanted | High |
| Currency (Spanish dollars) | Spanish silver dollar was the common currency across the Atlantic | One currency for all ports. Simple but historically faithful; no exchange-rate system needed | Low |
| Credit and bills of exchange | Bordeaux and Liverpool merchants financed trade with credit (Bordeaux sources) | Optional loans; a later-game system | High |

## 5. Later chapters (short; from general knowledge, not checked in this pass: unverified)

| Region | Signature goods | Main ports |
|---|---|---|
| Indian Ocean (c.1776-92) | Pepper (Malabar), cinnamon (Ceylon), cardamom, calico and cotton textiles (Coromandel, Bengal), indigo, saltpetre (Bengal, for gunpowder), coffee (Mocha), rice | Bombay, Madras, Calcutta, Surat, Cochin, Colombo, Mocha |
| Southeast Asia | Nutmeg, mace and cloves (Dutch monopoly in the Moluccas, smuggled), tin (Malaya, Bangka), pepper, rice, sugar, birds' nests and sea products | Batavia, Malacca, Penang (founded 1786), Bantam, Banda, Manila |
| East Asia | Tea, porcelain, silk, nankeen cloth, rhubarb; silver imported. Cotton from India | Canton (Guangzhou, the single legal port for the Western trade, via the Cohong), Macao |

Opium: the East India Company grew opium in Bengal and sold it to private "country" traders for the Canton market, which grew through the 1770s and 1780s. It was a real part of how Europeans financed the tea trade. Options: omit it and show a silver-drain pressure instead; or mention it in port text and a codex, without making it a tradeable good. Omission is the simplest and avoids an addictive-drug mechanic.
