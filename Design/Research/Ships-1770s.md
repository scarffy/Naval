# Ships of the 1770s: research for Naval

Scope: merchant ships around 1765-1780 for Chapter 1 (Atlantic, Gulf, Caribbean), plus short notes for Chapters 2-4. Accuracy target: names, roles and flavour, not simulation. Figures are typical ranges from general maritime history; web access during this pass was limited, so most numbers come from established background knowledge and are marked "(unverified)" where I could not check them against a source. Sources are listed at the end.

### 1b. Specific points

**Fluyt.** The fluyt was a Dutch invention (Hoorn, about 1590s) and dominated Dutch bulk trade in the 17th century. By 1770 it was mostly a Baltic, North Sea and Dutch coastal type, and the word was by then often a generic Dutch name for a freight ship ("fluit"). British, French and Spanish colonial trade used full-rigged ships, snows and brigs instead. I found no source for a precise end date; treat "still common in the 1770s Atlantic and colonial trade" as unlikely (unverified). Suggested replacement name for the large bulk carrier: **Merchantman / West Indiaman** (a full-rigged "ship"), or "Indiaman" for later chapters.

**Bermuda and Jamaica sloops.** Both were famous for speed and windward ability. Bermuda built thousands in the 18th century from fast-growing, durable Bermuda cedar (figures of about 1,000 to 4,000 sloops are quoted by different writers; unverified). They were used by traders, privateers, the Royal Navy and smugglers. A claim of 16.5 knots appears only on a game wiki, so I treat top speeds as unverified; "fastest small craft of the Atlantic" is the safe flavour. Jamaica sloops were similar, built of local cedar, and were used as privateers and for inter-island trade.

**New England schooners.** The schooner is usually credited to New England (Gloucester, Massachusetts, early 1700s) and to the Bermuda/Chesapeake tradition, and is the common coasting, fishing and West Indies vessel by 1770. Later "Baltimore clipper" schooners came from the Chesapeake (decline attributed to a shift to cargo capacity). Chapelle's *The Search for Speed Under Sail* is the standard reference.

**Snow vs brig vs brigantine.** All are two-masted. A brig has two square-rigged masts. A snow carries a small extra trysail mast right behind the main mast, and is usually larger. A brigantine has a square-rigged foremast and a fore-and-aft mainsail. Terminology was loose in practice, and varied by nation: in Spanish and French sources "bergantín" and "brigantin" often mean a small two-masted vessel that may be what English calls a brig. For the game, brig is the safe name.

**Packet ships.** Regular transatlantic liners (the Black Ball Line) are 19th century (1818). In the 1770s "packet" meant a mail vessel: the British Post Office packets from Falmouth to the West Indies and North America, and the Spanish correos marítimos (maritime mail, begun 1764) between La Coruña, Havana and elsewhere. These were brigs or snows, often armed.

**Copper sheathing.** The Royal Navy tested it on HMS Alarm in 1761; the problem of galvanic corrosion of iron bolts was solved in the 1780s, and the Navy re-bolted its fleet 1783-86. Merchant adoption was slow at first: one secondary source says about 275 vessels (3% of the British merchant fleet) were coppered by 1786 and 18% by 1816 (unverified, single secondary source). For 1770, coppered merchant ships should be rare: a few West Indiamen and East Indiamen, with wide use after about 1780.

## 2. Notes by region and port

**Gulf of Mexico and Mississippi**
- Natchez: flatboats, keelboats and pirogues on the river; occasionally small schooners or sloops from the Gulf reaching up to Natchez (British West Florida until 1779, then Spanish).
- New Orleans (Spanish since 1763-69): river craft, goletas and bergantines on the Gulf and to Havana; small coasters. Larger ships rarely came up the river at all (sandbars and the river mouth; unverified).
- Campeche: goletas and balandras (Spanish for small sloop-type), lugger-like local coasters carrying logwood and salt; small bergantines.
- Veracruz: register ships, packet correos, fragatas mercantes and bergantines; flota gone by 1778, when free trade rules opened more ports.

**Caribbean**
- Havana: major Spanish hub: register ships, fragatas, bergantines, goletas, and royal shipyard work (navíos). Packets from Spain.
- Port-au-Prince (French Saint-Domingue): senaux, brigantins, navires and goélettes carrying sugar and coffee to Bordeaux and Nantes; local sloops and smugglers. Armed.
- Bridgetown (British Barbados): West Indiamen, brigs, snows, and many New England schooners and sloops bringing food, lumber, and horses.

**North America**
- New York: sloops (Hudson River sloops), schooners, brigs, snows; trade to the West Indies and Europe.
- Boston: schooners, brigs, snows, ships; fishing fleet (schooners), lumber and rum trade; shipbuilding centre.

**Europe**
- Liverpool: full-rigged ships, snows, brigs, West Indiamen; Africa and slave-trade ships as well (notes for game: handle with care, unverified numbers).
- Bordeaux: navires, senaux, brigantins for Saint-Domingue and Martinique; also coasting gabares.
- Seville / Cádiz: Seville is up the Guadalquivir and silted; by 1770 Cádiz handled the Indies monopoly (to 1778). Fragatas mercantes, register ships, bergantines, xebecs/jabeques as coast and war craft, goletas, and tartanes (Mediterranean lateen-rigged cargo vessel).

## 4. Threats around 1770

**Piracy in the Caribbean.** The Golden Age of Piracy is usually dated to about 1650s-1730; the last big wave ended with the 1720s (Wikipedia and general summaries; sources agree). By 1770 organised pirate fleets were gone. Threats were small-scale: coastal robbery, wreckers, smugglers (contraband trade to Spanish colonies was endemic) and guarda costas (Spanish coast guard vessels that seized British ships). During the American Revolution (1775-83) privateers replaced pirates as the main danger, American, British, French (from 1778), Spanish (from 1779) and Dutch (from 1780). Letters of marque made it legal. This also matters for flavour: "pirates" in game can be framed as unlicensed or lapsed privateers.

**Gulf and Mississippi.** Low threat on the river itself: river hazards, river pirates in the American period are later (1790s, unverified). The Gulf: guarda costas, smugglers, British-Spanish rivalry (West Florida campaign 1779-81).

**North Atlantic.** Royal Navy press gangs and customs cutters (colonial smuggling disputes before 1775); American and British privateers after 1775. Weather is the big threat.

**Europe, Spain and the Mediterranean.** Barbary corsairs (Algiers, Tunis, Tripoli, Morocco) were still active in the 1770s. They used xebecs (fast, lateen-rigged, oared; also half-galleys and galiots); they hit Spanish, Italian and other shipping around the Strait of Gibraltar and the Spanish coast. Spain attacked Algiers in 1775 and failed (O'Reilly expedition), and bombarded it in 1783-84 (dates from general knowledge; unverified here). Seville/Cádiz-bound ships risked them near Cape St Vincent.

**How merchant ships were armed.** Typical merchantmen carried a handful of light guns (3-6 pounders; 4-12 guns for Atlantic ships; more for East and West Indiamen, and Africa ships), swivel guns, small arms, and boarding nettings. Small sloops and schooners carried swivels and muskets only. Armed merchantmen sailed in convoy in wartime. Letters-of-marque ships added guns and men.

## 5. Later chapters (short notes)

**Indian Ocean (about 1776).** East Indiamen (EIC): about 700-1,200 tons, with 100-130 crew and 26-30 guns (unverified range; common summary). Arab and Indian craft: baghlah (large deep-sea dhow, 200-400 tons, high carved stern), ghanjah (a large Indian/Arab ship, two or three masts), dhow (the generic lateen-rigged vessel: boom, sambuk), grab (Indian/Konkan coast vessel with a prow beak and two or three masts, used by Marathas and in Indian trade). Used by Gujarat, Oman, Malabar and Konkan merchants and navies.

**Southeast Asia (about 1784).** Bugis and Makassar craft: padewakang (large trading prau with tripod or lateen-like sails; carried trepang and cargo to northern Australia and across the archipelago). The pinisi rig (schooner-like with Western influence) is probably 19th century (unverified). Chinese junks (Amoy and Fujian) traded to Batavia, Manila and Siam. Spanish Manila: the Manila galleon (large, 1,000+ tons, to Acapulco; the route ran until 1815) and local Spanish ships.

**East Asia (about 1792).** Chinese junks: Fujian (Fuchuan) and Guangdong trading junks, three or more masts with battened sails and bulkheaded hulls (typically 200-800 tons; unverified). Dutch ships at Dejima: one or two VOC ships a year (small number allowed; Dutch only and Chinese at Nagasaki, plus Dutch factory on the artificial island).
